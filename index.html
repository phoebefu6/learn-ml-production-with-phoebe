ML in Production: The Leader Cut

Getting a model to work is the first half of an ML project. Keeping it worth running is the second, and it is decided by a handful of choices that are usually made by default: what problem to scope and what baseline to beat, how the model goes out to real traffic, when it gets retrained and on what, what gets watched and what happens when an alert fires, and when the right answer is to switch it off. Six sessions on those decisions, not the tooling. Each one carries a practitioner card for the team that will build what the leader signs. At the centre is a bench that replays two and a half years of a real electricity market in your browser under eight retrain policies, and prints what each one bought and what it cost.

The six sessions 🚢 · from the launch plan to the decision to stop

The running data is Elec2, 45,312 half-hours of the New South Wales electricity market from 1996 to 1998 and the most used concept-drift benchmark there is. The frameworks are Andrew Ng's Machine Learning in Production, the Hidden Technical Debt paper, the ML Test Score and Google's Rules of Machine Learning, each read at source.

Diligence on feasibility and on value before any data is touched, the baseline a model has to beat, metrics first, and the first rule of machine learning: do not be afraid to launch without it.

the scoping memo 🐤 Session 3 · advanced

Shadow, canary, or all at once

Degrees of automation from human only to full automation, choosing between shadow mode, canary and blue-green, and writing the rollback before the rollout. Why training and serving skew is the test teams skip.

the rollout plan 🎛️ Session 4 · bench night

The retrain-policy bench

15,104 real Elec2 rows replayed live under eight policies. Weekly refits buy 4.85 points over never for 130 retrains; an alarm buys 4.08 for 6; retraining before every prediction is worse than no model at all.

★ the bench 📟 Session 5 · advanced

Monitor what you will act on

Software, input and output signals, what to watch while labels are late, an action wired to every alert, and the feedback loops that let a model grade its own homework.

the alert-to-action table 🛑 Session 6 · advanced

When not to productionize

What the famous failure rates really say, the kill criteria you write before launch, and the capstone: a one-page production decision memo that can say ship, hold, or stop.

What is honest here 🧾

The bench runs on real public rows, recomputed in your browser. Frameworks are quoted from their primary sources. Where a claim comes second-hand, the page says so, and where a famous number has no source, the page says that instead.
